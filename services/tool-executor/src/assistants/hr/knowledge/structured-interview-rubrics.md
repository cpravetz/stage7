# Structured Interview Rubrics and Scoring Standards

This handbook is the design and scoring reference for the interview work the Human Resources & Talent Acquisition Assistant produces and consumes. It covers the validity evidence that justifies structured interviewing, the anatomy of a job-related competency rubric, the anchored rating scale the Draft Job Description & Interview Kit should emit, the rubric dimensions the Assess Candidate skill reports, and the administration rules that protect a structured process from quietly becoming an unstructured one. Every assessment produced in this assistant is an aid to a human rater; the rater's judgement governs the decision.

## Why structured interviewing works

The meta-analytic literature is the justification for how the kit is built. Across the classic analysis of predictor validity, structured interviews substantially outperform their unstructured counterparts, and work-sample tests and cognitive ability tests sit alongside them at the top:

| Selection method | Typical validity coefficient | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Work sample / job knowledge test | roughly .54 | Highest, but narrowest construct coverage |
| Structured interview | roughly .51 | Strong generalisability across roles and cultures |
| Cognitive ability test | roughly .51 | Strong, but adverse-impact exposure |
| Unstructured interview | roughly .38 | Ratings dominated by rater effects |
| Assessment centre | roughly .37 to .46 | Costly; strong for senior and executive roles |
| Years of experience | roughly .18 | Weak alone; useful as a filter at most |
| Education level | roughly .10 | Weak; rarely job-related on its own |
| Unstructured biodata | roughly .10 to .20 | Requires a validated instrument |
| Interests | roughly .10 to .20 | Weak predictor of performance |

Two caveats worth carrying. Later meta-analyses have challenged some of the classic corrections applied to these figures downward, and the honest summary is that structured interviewing sits in the top tier alongside work samples and cognitive testing, not unambiguously above it. And validity figures are population and job dependent; a design that has not been validated locally should be treated as provisional.

The practical mechanism is not the questions. It is that all candidates in a role answer the same questions, the same questions are asked of every candidate, ratings are made against pre-defined anchors rather than overall impression, raters record evidence before discussing, and scores are combined only after independent rating. Remove any of those and the design degrades toward unstructured interviewing regardless of how behavioural the question wording looks.

## Job analysis to competency

Structured interviewing starts one step before question writing. The chain is:

1. **Job analysis.** Document the essential functions from observation and incumbent input, not from the existing incumbent's habits.
2. **Competency model.** Convert essential functions into a small set of competencies — four to six per role is the usable range. Each needs an observable definition.
3. **Behavioural indicators.** For each competency, write what strong, adequate, and weak performance looks like in observable behaviour.
4. **Questions.** Probe for the competencies, using past behaviour as the evidence source.
5. **Rating scale.** Anchor each level, define the evidence required to assign it.
6. **Administration.** Standardise who asks what, capture evidence before the debrief, and combine scores by a pre-agreed rule.

Reverse-rating is the discipline that keeps step 3 honest. For every competency, ask what an exemplary, an adequate, and a poor performer would each look like. If you cannot write the poor column, the competency is too vague to assess.

Competencies in current assessment practice tend toward a short list of cross-functional constructs — problem solving, communication, collaboration, adaptability, judgement, and role-specific technical depth — rather than a long list of task descriptions. Task lists are covered by a work sample and are not what an interview should be measuring.

## The anchored rating scale

A five-point anchored scale is the right default for competency scoring, and a "not observed" option must sit outside the numeric range. Scoring an unobserved behaviour as a 1 conflates absence of evidence with evidence of absence, which is the most common way structured interviews degrade.

| Anchor | Label | Definition | Typical evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | Exceptional | Clearly exceeds the requirement; demonstrates the behaviour at a level beyond the role | Specific example with measurable outcome, sustained across contexts |
| 4 | Strong | Consistently meets the requirement at the target level | Specific example with outcome and reflection |
| 3 | Adequate | Meets the requirement adequately in most instances | One relevant example; some gaps or narrow evidence |
| 2 | Developing | Partially meets the requirement; needs support in some contexts | Vague, partial, or second-hand example |
| 1 | Insufficient | Does not meet the requirement | Example of the behaviour, or contradiction of the standard |
| N/O | Not observed | No opportunity to observe | No evidence gathered — do not convert to a numeric score |

Rules that keep the scale honest:

- Score the evidence present, not the impression formed. An enthusiastic delivery does not raise a score.
- Score against the anchor text, not against other candidates. Anchoring on the panel is the most common rater error and it is why independent rating happens before any discussion.
- Write the note at the moment of the answer. Recall at debrief time reconstructs what was said rather than what was observed.
- A `2` or `1` requires a written reason. It is the score that gets challenged later.

## Weights and combination

Weights are set at design time and applied mechanically. Default distribution for a generalist role:

| Competency | Default weight | Rater |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Role-specific technical or functional depth | 35 percent | Specialist interviewer |
| Problem solving and judgement | 20 percent | Hiring manager |
| Communication | 15 percent | Hiring manager |
| Collaboration and teamwork | 15 percent | Peer or cross-functional interviewer |
| Adaptability and learning agility | 15 percent | Any panel member |

Sum to 100 percent. Executive and senior roles shift weight toward judgement, leadership, and the ability to assess the decision context; entry roles shift toward problem solving, communication, and learning agility. Weights are declared per competency on the scorecard, and the draft kit is what the panel sees before interviewing — a weight decided after the debriefs are in is not a weight.

Aggregate in one of two ways: a weighted mean across competencies per interviewer, or a competency-by-competency mean across interviewers. The second is preferable, because it surfaces the case where one interviewer is an outlier on a specific competency rather than letting that interviewer move the whole score.

## Behavioural question construction

The question stem format that produces usable evidence:

> "Tell me about a time when [situation]. What was your specific contribution? What did you do, and what was the outcome?"

Every part matters. The past-behaviour cue anchors the question in evidence that already happened. Asking for the candidate's specific contribution separates it from the team's. The follow-up on what *they* did prevents delegation credit. The outcome request is what turns a story into a performance measurement.

Follow-ups are what carry the weight. Each rubric dimension needs one scripted probe:

- Depth: "What alternatives did you consider, and why did you reject them?"
- Specificity: "Who else was involved, and what did they do?"
- Result: "How do you know it worked? What was measured?"
- Reflection: "What would you do differently now?"
- Contradiction: "You described this as your decision, but mentioned the lead approved it — how did you reach it?"

Also required in every behavioural question:

- A competency tag, so the score maps to a rubric row.
- A probe list, so the panel is not inventing questions live.
- A **negative indicator**: what a concerning answer would look like for this competency. This is the most under-authored part of most interview kits and it is what gives a rater the confidence to record a low score.
- Standard positioning wording, so difficulty of the probe does not vary by interviewer.

## Rater error catalogue

Structured design suppresses these errors; it does not eliminate them. Reviewers should look for them when auditing a scorecard set.

| Error | Description | Mitigation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leniency or severity | One rater scores systematically higher or lower | Independent rating, then cross-interviewer calibration before the debrief |
| Halo or horns | A strong impression on one trait carries across all traits | Score one competency fully before moving to the next |
| Contrast | Judged against the previous candidate rather than the anchors | Fixed question set, no candidate order discussion |
| Recency | The last answer dominates | Immediate written notes per competency |
| Similarity | Bonus for a background resembling the rater's | Anchor the scale, remove the rater from decision-making if they share the background |
| Central tendency | Avoiding extremes, especially with the 5-point scale | Require justification for every score outside 3 |
| Contrast effect in panel | Late speakers conform to the consensus | Score independently and in writing before any discussion |

## Designing the kit the assistant emits

The Draft Job Description & Interview Kit skill generates structured job descriptions, interview scorecards, role-specific behavioural questions, and rubric guides, delivered to the recruiting-ops endpoint. The standard it should hold to:

- Competencies are behavioural and role-specific, not generic personality adjectives. "Persuasive when presenting to stakeholders" is testable; "outgoing" is not.
- Every competency carries the reverse-rated three-column definition.
- Every behavioural question carries a competency tag, probes, and a negative indicator.
- Every scorecard carries anchors, a not-observed option, a weight, and a written-reason requirement for the lowest anchors.
- The job description lists essential functions separately from preferred qualifications, and does not use a degree requirement or a specific school or employer name unless a business necessity is documented. Undocumented "prestige signalling" requirements are a standard disparate-impact finding.
- The kit states that the panel is scored against the rubric and that panel composition is intentional rather than whoever is free.

## What the assessment skills actually report

Two skills produce assessment numbers, and both are keyword-based. They must be read as what they are.

**Screen Resume for Role Fit** tokenises the resume and the requirements, counts exact token matches, adds partial credit for requirement tokens of three or more characters that appear as a substring of a resume token, and reports `score` out of 100 with `matched`, `partialMatched`, and `gaps`. The score is floored at 1 rather than 0, so a candidate with no matches still receives a non-zero number. Treat it as an attention-directing keyword-coverage measure. It is not a merit measure, and the substring matching is what makes it fragile — a requirement token of four characters will match inside unrelated words.

**Assess Candidate Skills and Experience** reports `yearsExperience` from a single pattern matching a number followed by "year" or "yr" — so it reads the first mention in the document, not the candidate's total career — plus `technicalSkillMatch` and `softSkillMatch`, each with `total`, `found`, and `missing`, and an `experienceMentioned` boolean. Its output line states that the source is local keyword analysis with no external API and that every figure is computed from what was supplied.

Neither number belongs in an employment decision, and neither should appear in a rubric row. Their legitimate use is to direct a human reader's attention: a technical skill listed as missing is a question to ask about, not a finding of absence.

## Interview scheduling as a process control

Interview design is undermined by scheduling noise, because the best-prepared candidate drops out when slots are unusable. The automated scheduling skill therefore treats its policy as configuration rather than input: a required `calendarId` scopes every booking, `roundTypes` lists the rounds the skill is permitted to book, and `timeWindowRules` carries `timezone`, `durationMinutes`, `notBefore`, and `notAfter`. A screening event cannot talk the skill into booking a round the operator did not enable — an unconfigured round returns a skipped status with the round named.

Operating rules that follow: offer times in at least two time zones plus remote, honour accessibility needs in scheduling rather than as an afterthought, keep candidate wait times under a day by assigning an interviewer owner rather than a queue, and send every candidate the same information regardless of stage.

## Configuring this

The five-point scale, the not-observed option, the default weight distribution, the low-anchor justification rule, and the independent-rating-before-debrief rule are the defaults this assistant applies. Weights and anchor definitions are generated into each kit and reviewed by the hiring manager, so a role-specific weight set is a kit change rather than a configuration change. The scheduling policy fields — `calendarId`, `roundTypes`, `timeWindowRules` — are persisted per deployment, as are the dry-run default, the confirmation requirement for live dispatch, and the request rate limit and retry counts. The keyword-analysis limits in the screening and assessment skills are inherent to their arithmetic and are not tunable; anything that needs a validity-backed measure belongs in the interview itself.
